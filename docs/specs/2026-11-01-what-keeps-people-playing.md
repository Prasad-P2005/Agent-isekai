# What keeps people playing, and what this game does about it

Research for the update of 2026-11-01. Each principle says what the source found, how strong that source is, and what it became here. Where a source is a blog or a press summary, it says so.

## The principles

### 1. Competence comes first

Ryan, Rigby and Przybylski (2006) ran four studies on why games pull at people. Perceived autonomy and competence went with enjoyment, preference for a game, and better well-being after play. Autonomy, competence and relatedness each independently predicted enjoyment and future play in their online community study.

One result stands out. In Study 1, both needs predicted enjoyment, but **only competence predicted whether people chose to carry on with the game** when offered another. Their route to competence is specific: controls that are intuitive and readily mastered, tasks that are ongoing optimal challenges, and positive feedback.

Here: the hand, the sound the game never had, the hit marker, and a director that watches how the hero is doing.

### 2. Autonomy means real choices

The same paper ties autonomy to enjoyment. An Iowa State experiment on Minecraft (352 people) found the freest players scored highest on creativity afterwards, and those told to be creative scored lowest. It suggests being told can narrow what people do. That study measured creativity rather than engagement, so it only supports this indirectly.

Here: Laws are chosen, not assigned. The god suggests and never orders. Nothing about the story has a clock on it, apart from a siege, which can be lost.

### 3. Relatedness: somebody notices

Relatedness independently predicted enjoyment and future play (Ryan et al., Study 4, in multiplayer players). This game is single player, so the only stand-in is characters who react. Supergiant's Hades checks the state of the game (health below a threshold, for example) and picks from pre-written lines that fit, weighted so the order stays unpredictable. Greg Kasavin describes the script as built around what characters would notice. Note the leap: Ryan measured real people and Hades supplies written ones, so this is the design inference, not the finding.

Here: the god is the relationship. She reacts to what actually happened, confides more as Faith rises, and residents talk about the Laws the hero chose.

### 4. Flow needs clear goals, instant feedback and fitting challenge

Jenova Chen (2007): challenge beyond ability causes anxiety, too little loses the player, and players' flow zones differ, so one static difficulty fails. His method: offer adaptive choices, and **embed them in the core activity so flow is never interrupted**. He warns that too many choices overwhelm, and that being forced into frequent choices annoys. Researchers do question the simple challenge-skill model, so treat it as a guide.

Here: three Law cards, never a popup. The player opens them when they want to. Choosing a Law's second line is choosing a difficulty, which is Chen's adaptive choice inside play. The director eases off when the hero is struggling.

### 5. People speed up near a goal, and a head start helps

Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng (2006): effort depends on the *proportion* of distance left to a reward, and a coffee card with two pre-stamped bonus stamps was finished faster than a plain card needing the same purchases. The tendency to speed up towards the *first* reward predicted retention. Nunes and Dreze (2006) found the same in car washes: 34% finished a 10-stamp card with 2 free stamps against 19% on an 8-stamp card, with the same eight purchases needed.

Here: one Faith bar that starts a fifth full at every level, with the first reward a few minutes in.

### 6. Unfinished things stay on the mind

Zeigarnik (1927) found interrupted tasks are better remembered, and Rigby and Ryan point to MMOs offering a new quest as one is handed in. The effect is not fully reliable and depends on motivation, so this is used lightly.

Here: finishing one thing leaves another visible. The compass moves to the next stone, and a level leaves a Law waiting.

### 7. Variable reward on top of a fixed floor

Variable-ratio schedules are described as giving the highest, steadiest response. The sourcing is weaker than it sounds, mostly design writing plus Wikipedia, and the same writing warns that overuse burns players out and can undermine intrinsic motivation.

Here: effort always pays a known amount of Faith. What varies is what turns up on top: which three Laws are offered, falling stars and what they hold, ore luck. Nothing that matters is locked behind a roll.

### 8. Show how close it was, honestly

A University of Portsmouth article on Vampire Survivors describes near misses: a run that fails may still have come close to a level or a chest. Reports credit its pull to how often it pays the player.

Here: dying says how much Faith was left to the next Law, when that is close. It is always the true number.

### 9. Failure must not waste time

Supergiant set out to "take the pain out of dying and having to restart", so that a death would not make people quit, and made the story advance through deaths. A Saskatchewan thesis (2021, 300 participants, not peer reviewed) found loss aversion holds even for in-game gold.

Here: dying costs no Faith, levels or items. It feeds the story: the god reacts to how it happened, and the killer gets a name. The one thing at stake is the run of nights survived, which is enough tension without a wasted hour.

### 10. Juice is layered, small, and fades

Jonasson and Purho ("Juice it or lose it", 2012) and Nijman ("The art of screenshake", 2013) built plain games up with layers of feedback. Practical advice agrees on several small responses within about 100 ms, a brief hit-stop, and shake that is short and not on every hit, because players tune it out. Most of those details come from secondary write-ups, not the talks.

Here: the game had **no sound at all**, which is the biggest gap this research exposed. Now a hit has a sound, a marker and a flash. Only kills get the kick and the hit-stop. Particle budgets stay as they were, and reduced-motion is respected.

### 11. Less friction, less text

Reports on Vampire Survivors stress low friction. Celia Hodent notes that players skip long text or scan it, and that motivation is the origin of behaviour.

Here: one line at a time in a small talk box, with the world still in view. One objective, one compass.

## What was left out on purpose

Zagal, Björk and Lewis call these dark patterns, and a 2024 study of mobile games names grinding and playing by appointment among them. Deterding, Stenros and Montola point out the framing itself is debated, but the patterns are well described.

So: no real-time timers, no daily logins, no content that vanishes for good, nothing to buy. Everything runs on the game clock, so pausing pauses it. A streak counts nights in the game, not days in the real world. Przybylski et al. (2009) separate wanting to play from having to play. I could only read the opening of that paper, so the finding is not relied on here, but it is the same line this list draws.

## How it fits together

```
deeds -> Faith bar -> a level -> a Law to write (three choices) -> the world plays differently -> new deeds
```

Around that sit a nightly rhythm (survive to dawn, and the streak grows), the eleven stones that lead to a fourth ending, and a director that alternates pressure with relief.
